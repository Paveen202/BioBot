50 Tips for Conservation

Practical ways to protect plants and wildlife, save water and energy, and care for the places we share. Start with one action that fits your life.

Explore the tips ↓

Four connected priorities

Protect plants. Save water. Use power wisely. Care for nature.

The 50 tips below include small actions for each of these shared needs.

🌱PlantsSupport local plant life.💧WaterUse fresh water thoughtfully.⚡PowerReduce avoidable energy use.🌳NatureProtect connected habitats.

01 — 10

Make space for wildlife

Small choices at home and outdoors can make local habitats safer and more welcoming.

  1. Plant native trees, shrubs, and flowers suited to your region.
  2. Choose a mix of native plants that bloom or fruit in different seasons.
  3. Replace a small part of a lawn with a wildlife-friendly garden.
  4. Leave some fallen leaves in quiet garden spots for insects and soil life.
  5. Avoid pesticides where possible; ask local experts about safer options.
  6. Remove invasive plants using guidance from a trusted local source.
  7. Keep cats indoors or in a secure outdoor enclosure to protect wildlife.
  8. Make large windows easier for birds to see with external markers or screens.
  9. Turn off outdoor lights when they are not needed.
  10. Observe wild animals from a distance and never feed them.

11 — 20

Use energy and water wisely

Using fewer resources can reduce pollution and pressure on natural systems.

  1. Switch off lights when you leave a room.
  2. Choose energy-efficient LED bulbs when replacing old ones.
  3. Walk, cycle, or take public transport when it works for your trip.
  4. Combine errands to reduce the number of car journeys.
  5. Turn off the engine when parked and waiting.
  6. Air-dry clothes when weather and space allow.
  7. Wash full loads of clothes, using a cool setting when suitable.
  8. Fix dripping taps and leaks promptly.
  9. Take shorter showers and turn off the tap while brushing your teeth.
  10. Carry a reusable water bottle or cup.

21 — 30

Reduce waste and pollution

Buying thoughtfully and reusing what you have can keep useful materials out of nature.

  1. Bring a reusable bag when you shop.
  2. Plan purchases so you buy only what you will use.
  3. Repair clothes, toys, and electronics before replacing them.
  4. Use refillable containers for lunches and snacks.
  5. Choose reusable products when they are practical for you.
  6. Compost food scraps if a safe local option is available.
  7. Sort recycling according to your local collection rules.
  8. Take batteries and electronics to an approved collection point.
  9. Put litter in a bin, or join a local cleanup if it is safe to do so.
  10. Avoid releasing balloons or plastic decorations outdoors.

31 — 40

Choose food thoughtfully

Food choices can support healthier soils, lower waste, and responsible fishing and farming.

  1. Plan meals and portions to help prevent food waste.
  2. Store food well and use older ingredients first.
  3. Try more plant-rich meals if that suits your needs.
  4. Choose seasonal produce when it is available and affordable.
  5. Check trusted local guidance when buying seafood.
  6. Avoid products made from threatened wildlife or illegally traded species.
  7. Look for credible sustainability information on wood and paper products.
  8. Support farms and food businesses that share responsible practices.
  9. Grow herbs or vegetables at home or in a community garden if you can.
  10. Freeze or share extra food before it goes to waste.

41 — 50

Help your community protect nature

Conservation grows when people learn, work together, and care for local places.

  1. Follow park rules and leave natural places as you found them.
  2. Stay on marked trails to protect plants and nesting areas.
  3. Keep dogs leashed where local rules or wildlife needs call for it.
  4. Do not move firewood between areas; it can spread pests and disease.
  5. Clean shoes and outdoor gear to avoid carrying seeds between habitats.
  6. Volunteer for a local habitat restoration or planting day.
  7. Support a conservation group or nature center you trust.
  8. Join a reputable community science project and follow its instructions.
  9. Check sources before sharing wildlife or conservation claims.
  10. Encourage local decisions that protect parks, rivers, and wildlife habitat.

Many causes · connected effects

Pollution is more than littering

Smoke, dirty water, chemicals, noise, and excessive light can all pollute the environment. Some pollutants come from natural events, but human activities can create more, spread them, or make their effects last longer.

Poaching and overhunting harm nature too.They are usually classed as direct exploitation of wildlife, not pollution. Careless actions can cause pollution and other damage to habitats.

01 · Air

Air pollution

Sources: Vehicle exhaust, power plants, industry, cooking and heating fuels, and burning waste. Wildfires, dust storms, and volcanoes can also release smoke, particles, or gases.

Effects: Polluted air can harm people, wildlife, crops, forests, and climate. Some pollutants settle onto soil and water.

02 · Freshwater and oceans

Water pollution

Sources: Untreated sewage, industrial discharges, oil and fuel spills, mining waste, farm runoff, and litter carried by drains and rivers. Natural erosion can also add sediment.

Effects: Contaminants can poison aquatic life, damage habitats, and make water unsafe for people and animals.

03 · Land and soil

Soil pollution

Sources: Pesticides, excess fertilizers, mining, leaking fuel or chemicals, and poorly managed rubbish. Some harmful elements also occur naturally in rocks and soil.

Effects: Contaminated soil can harm plants and soil organisms, and some pollutants can move into groundwater or food chains.

04 · Waste

Plastic and solid-waste pollution

Sources: Single-use packaging, discarded fishing gear, clothing fibers, electronics, and waste that is not collected or safely managed.

Effects: Animals can become entangled in debris or mistake it for food. Plastic breaks into smaller pieces and can persist in ecosystems.

05 · Harmful substances

Chemical pollution

Sources: Some industrial chemicals, pesticides, solvents, medicines, and metals released through manufacturing, farming, mining, or unsafe disposal.

Effects: Certain chemicals can remain in the environment, harm organisms, or build up through food webs. Chemical pollution can affect air, water, and soil.

06 · Excess nutrients

Nutrient pollution

Sources: Too much nitrogen or phosphorus from fertilizers, manure, and wastewater washing into lakes, rivers, and coastal waters.

Effects: Excess nutrients can fuel harmful algal blooms. As blooms die and decompose, oxygen can fall and fish or other aquatic life may be harmed.

07 · Sound

Noise pollution

Sources: Road and air traffic, construction, ships, and some industrial activities. Storms and animals also make natural sounds; pollution is disruptive human-made noise.

Effects: Loud or persistent noise can interfere with wildlife communication, finding food, avoiding danger, and moving through habitats.

08 · Artificial light

Light pollution

Sources: Bright, misdirected, or unnecessary outdoor lighting from buildings, roads, signs, and sports areas.

Effects: Artificial light at night can confuse migrating birds and insects and disrupt natural day-and-night patterns for wildlife.

09 · Heat

Thermal pollution

Sources: Warm water released by some industrial processes, or loss of tree shade along streams and rivers.

Effects: Rapid changes in water temperature can stress aquatic species and reduce the oxygen available to them.

10 · Radioactive material

Radioactive contamination

Sources: Improperly managed radioactive waste, some mining and industrial processes, or accidents. Small amounts of naturally occurring radioactive materials are also found in rocks and soil.

Effects: If radioactive material is released, it can contaminate air, land, or water and require careful monitoring and cleanup.

Other human pressures on nature

Not all environmental harm is pollution

🐾

Poaching and illegal wildlife trade

Taking protected animals or plants from the wild can drive populations down and disrupt ecosystems.

🦌

Overhunting and overfishing

Removing wildlife faster than populations can recover can upset food webs and threaten species.

🌱

Careless actions

Leaving fishing line or rubbish, dumping chemicals, disturbing nests, or damaging habitat can hurt wildlife. Some actions cause pollution; others cause direct harm.

🌳

Habitat destruction

Clearing or fragmenting forests, wetlands, grasslands, and reefs removes shelter, food, and breeding places.

Small actions and bigger changes

How can we help?

  • Use less disposable packaging, reuse what you can, and sort waste according to local rules.
  • Never pour oil, paint, medicines, or chemicals down drains; use local safe-disposal programs.
  • Switch off unused lights and devices, save energy, and choose cleaner transport when practical to reduce pollution from burning fuels.
  • Use garden chemicals carefully and follow local guidance on fertilizers and pesticides.
  • Keep outdoor lights pointed downward and switch them off when not needed.
  • Respect wildlife: keep a safe distance, do not buy products made from threatened species, and report suspected wildlife crime to local authorities.
  • Use water thoughtfully and support clean water, native plant life, wildlife protection, and responsible waste-management efforts in your community.

Protect the systems that keep nature healthy

Pollution prevention is part of conservation

Keeping waterways clean, protecting soil and plant life, and reducing pollution from energy use all help wildlife. Saving water and electricity also reduces pressure on the natural systems that supply them.

Understand the risk · protect people and nature

Natural Hazards & Disaster Risk Reduction

Earthquakes, storms, floods, and droughts are natural hazards. We may not be able to stop them, but we can make disasters less likely and reduce the harm they cause.

A hazard is not automatically a disaster.Disaster risk rises when a hazard affects people or ecosystems that are exposed and vulnerable, especially when they have fewer ways to prepare or recover.

01 · Earth processes

Geological hazards

Earthquakes happen when stress along faults is released as the ground moves. Volcanoes erupt when magma and gases rise through the Earth's crust. Steep slopes can also fail as landslides, sometimes after heavy rain or shaking.

02 · Weather and water

Weather-related hazards

Storms, extreme heat, drought, and heavy rainfall can trigger floods and wildfires. Natural climate patterns shape these events, while human-caused climate change can worsen some climate-related hazards.

03 · People and places

Why impacts differ

Where homes are built, building strength, access to warnings, health, income, and emergency services all affect how severely people are harmed. Losing wetlands, forests, or other natural buffers can increase some local risks.

04 · Nature and wildlife

Why conservation matters

Healthy ecosystems can help communities cope with some hazards: wetlands can store floodwater, and coastal mangroves can reduce wave energy. Protecting habitat also gives wildlife more room and safer places during disturbance.

Reduce risk before a disaster

What can we do?

  • Use early-warning systems. Make sure alerts reach everyone and communities know what to do.
  • Build and plan safely. Use hazard maps, strong building standards, and land-use plans that avoid high-risk areas where possible.
  • Prepare together. Practice evacuation plans, identify safe places, and check on neighbors who may need help.
  • Protect natural buffers. Conserve forests, wetlands, dunes, and mangroves. Where local experts recommend restoration, use suitable native plants and respect local land and water guidance. These buffers can reduce some risks, but they do not prevent every disaster.
  • Save energy and reduce climate risks. Use power thoughtfully and support cleaner energy choices where available. Cutting greenhouse-gas pollution can limit longer-term climate risks, while communities still need to prepare for heat, drought, and heavier rainfall.
  • Conserve freshwater. Use water carefully, protect rivers and wetlands from pollution, and follow local drought guidance.
  • Follow trusted local guidance. Emergency services and local authorities know the hazards and plans for your area.

Healthy plants, soils, rivers, and wetlands support both wildlife and communities. Nature-based actions can complement safe planning and infrastructure; they are not a substitute for warnings or emergency preparation. The goal is disaster risk reduction: prevent new risks, reduce existing risks, and strengthen communities so they can respond and recover.
